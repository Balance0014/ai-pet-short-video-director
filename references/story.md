# Story and duration

Read when developing or diagnosing a story. The goal is one strong film, not a pile of candidate titles.

## Make the proposition visible

Find a surprising opening image, a familiar need, and a choice particular to this pet's way of noticing and moving. Ask: **What visibly changes because this animal chooses to act?** If the answer needs “actually,” “symbolizes,” hidden backstory, or a narrator, re-stage the mechanism.

Use this causal chain as a diagnostic, not a mandatory shot count:

`start state → disruption → obstruction or first failed approach → changed method or costly choice → visible result → emotional, relational, or comic return`

An unusual world rule is welcome when a viewer can understand it from its first visible effect. Avoid two unfamiliar rules that must be decoded together. Put originality in the premise, the pet's choice, or the ending—not in an unreadable explanation. Keep species-specific behavior and body mechanics recognizable unless a new ability is established on screen.

## Choose a profile

**Compact short.** Keep only indispensable changes. A 15-second target often supports a visible setup, one obstruction or reversal, one active decision, a result, and a brief reaction. Budget the viewer's time to understand the result. If the complete chain does not fit, simplify the mechanism or propose a longer film; do not hide causal steps in fast cuts or voice-over.

**Rescue short.** Establish who is at risk, why the threat is immediate, and why the subject cannot simply leave. Show an obstruction, the protagonist's distinct intervention, the danger-to-safety change, and a payoff that credits the protagonist. Reject graphic suffering, repetitive danger skins, an off-screen rescue, or an ending consisting only of applause or tears. Never label fictional generated footage as a real incident.

**Extended short.** Give extra time only to new information: a failed method, escalation, cost, changed relationship, uncertain climax, or meaningful aftermath. If the middle repeats movement or waiting, the film has not earned the length.

The user's tool may have a per-generation limit different from the final film's duration. Split source generation at a stable completed action, then edit to the intended final runtime. Verify current tool limits when they affect the plan.

## Greenlight test

Before presenting a story, check:

- **Hook:** Would the first image make a viewer ask what happens next?
- **Readability:** Can a viewer explain the visible cause and result without a production briefing?
- **Agency:** What choice makes this pet, with its particular body and temperament, irreplaceable?
- **Novelty:** Would changing only the animal, prop, or location leave the same story?
- **Emotion:** Which visible beat earns relief, laughter, surprise, being chosen, reunion, or another specific feeling?
- **Feasibility:** Which action is most likely to be misread or overload the chosen generation workflow?
- **Duration:** Does the complete action and reaction fit, including the ending?

If a recurring series has a history, compare the mechanism and payoff against prior episodes. Do not require a database when none exists. Do not claim that an internal numerical score predicts views or follows.

## Pitch to the user

Return one strongest option unless alternatives were requested: title, one-line hook, four to six visible beats, the protagonist-only choice, final audience feeling and its visible cause, proposed runtime, and the one production risk to solve next. Keep camera jargon out of story approval; the user should be able to picture the film first.
