# Direction, coverage, and continuity

Read after the story is approved, or when the user specifically requests shot design.

## Design backward from the cut

For each source shot, know the intended final beat, useful seconds, head and tail handles, and neighboring shot. More angles are only useful when they add a new observation, reaction, obstacle, or edit choice. The final film may use only part of a generated clip.

Block before framing: the pet's starting posture, paws and head, target and obstacle, route through space, eyelines, prop contact, reaction, and exact end position. Preserve credible weight, gait, and contact for the animal's species. Then select shot size and camera behavior. Use a stable camera when movement adds no information.

## Shot card

`story function | first visual focus | start state | stimulus → reaction → decision → action | blocking and camera | secondary world response | sound | end state | continuity anchors | likely failure`

The first focus should remain clear. A person, animal, object, weather effect, or light change outside the main action should contribute pressure, consequence, humor, or relationship information. Do not choreograph every element to move at once.

For a causal prop, trace `first appearance → owner/noticer → mover → handoff or travel → effect → final state`. If the route is invisible and cannot be understood, show the step, simplify the prop, or change the duration.

## Keyframe choice

- **First frame:** simple action within stable geography.
- **First and last frames:** important transformation, scale change, exact action endpoint, or a designed cut.
- **Small storyboard or spatial sketch:** camera position, occlusion, sight lines, object scale, or hidden mechanism determine whether the action is readable.

When a character-to-object connection is the point of the scene, make that connection visible in a reference image. A separate character portrait plus a verbal instruction may not fix a precise attachment or entrance. Inspect reference images for duplicates, look-alikes, reflections, shadows, posters, or toys that might become extra characters.

For cross-shot continuity, record recurring identity, costume or fur, geography, screen direction, prop state, light direction, weather, and sound. Start a continuation from the last **accepted real image** and describe off-screen facts in text. A tail frame alone cannot preserve a character who is outside the frame.

## Performance and sound

Replace emotion labels with a visible sequence: stimulus, body reaction, pause or look, choice, action. Use animal-specific evidence such as gaze, ears, tail, breathing, paw placement, weight shift, and distance from another subject; do not make every species act like a dog. Inner voice can reveal a judgment or relationship the picture cannot express; remove it when it merely narrates the action.

Plan dialogue, ambience, action sound, animal vocalization, music, and silence separately. “No background music” removes music, not useful dialogue or environmental sound. A sound cue should support an event or transition, not compensate for an invisible cause.

## Director's preflight

Before a paid prompt, answer: Can the audience see the intended cause? Is the main action unobstructed? Can the lead physically perform it, or is the exceptional rule already established? Is the result visibly complete? Can the next shot start from this exact end state? If geometry is uncertain, make the smallest useful sketch or still pair rather than a full animation.
